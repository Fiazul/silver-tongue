# Silver Tongue: merchant conversation UX research

Research date: 2026-10-14. Scope: first-party game descriptions, accessibility guidance, and primary language-learning studies. These establish implemented patterns or limited research findings; they do not validate Silver Tongue's proposed combination. No source below establishes an optimal online-time threshold, preparation window, merchant stay, rival difficulty, or weekly schedule.

Current user direction: a merchant visits about once per in-game week, stays one in-game day (roughly 30 minutes of play), and gives 5–15 minutes of advance notice. A 10-minute preparation window is a proposed starting value for testing. The existing day advances through completed scenes; translating that into active-play minutes is a separate game-clock design decision. Distinguish weekly recurrence, visit availability, preparation and any conversation rush timer in both implementation and player-facing explanations.

## Recommended design hypotheses to test

1. **Compete on the offer.** A rival's tempting price is an obstacle; learning what the merchant values unlocks another route to victory. Quantity, reliability, delivery time and relationship can matter alongside price. Winning is reaching the stated deal goal, rather than passing a grammar score.
2. **Give preparation a purpose.** The incoming notice identifies the location, opportunity category, stakes and rival without promising particular stock. During the user-requested 5–15 minutes (start testing at 10), players can inspect stock, ask townspeople, discover one useful clue, and rehearse without spending bargaining resources. Do not require idle waiting after preparation; test an optional advance-to-arrival action.
3. **Make text actionable.** Retain free-text typing. Optional intent hints such as “ask about quantity” reveal possible moves without selecting the player's final sentence. Interpret understandable intent; clarify ambiguous numbers or terms before changing the offer. Keep the current offer visible after each consequential exchange.
4. **Locate rush in the world.** Arrival countdown, a competing bid or impending departure create urgency. Separately allow deliberate reading and accessible composition. Offer standard, extended and untimed/practice settings without disabling story progression; pause gameplay time during loading and technical retries. Whether standard drafting itself runs the clock needs playtesting.
5. **Make setbacks useful.** A rival can win the best consignment while the player retains a smaller deal, a relationship gain or new information. Reveal the actual reason and the next opportunity. This is our proposal; the research above does not establish an optimal failure penalty or prove that failure improves learning.
6. **Change the situation, not just the required sentence.** Begin with town rivals, then introduce adventurers with expedition constraints. Rotate scarcity, bulk purchasing, quality concerns, delivery guarantees and reputation; let each create a different information gap and negotiation strategy.
7. **Explain why the player needs the language through an early action.** Start with a small town problem, show how asking an NPC changes its outcome, then connect the merchant's trade to the larger journey. Teach movement, interacting, typing, clarification and offer confirmation when each becomes useful. Keep the next objective visible and offer an optional story recap. First-town completion should mean solving the town problem and unlocking the next route, with another chance after a missed visit. These are proposed onboarding choices informed by the narrative game examples and accessibility guidance above, not a demonstrated retention intervention.

Latest conversation requirement: merchant goods or needs change materially on every completed subsequent encounter, including within one visit. An old furniture request may already be filled by a rival; the merchant should acknowledge it, explain the change, and expose a useful current need. This exact combination is Silver Tongue's proposed design, not a solved pattern established by the sources above. Persist prior requests, changed stock and committed terms; keep questions inside one ongoing deal stable.

The [flow plan](superpowers/specs/2026-10-14-first-town-conversation-flow.md) selects the existing action-based calendar for the first lab: two preparation activities target approximately 10 minutes, and four market-day slots target approximately 30 minutes. These are play-duration targets rather than an implemented active-time countdown. It places rush in opportunity budgets and rival progression while leaving reading/composing at the player's pace.

## What the next lab should measure

- Can a novice explain the arrival time, destination, useful preparation action, current offer and win condition without facilitator explanation?
- Does the player try to solve the merchant's problem, or hunt for a hidden correct sentence? Can two different understandable offers produce coherent outcomes?
- Do phrase hints support composing, or become compulsory answer selection? Can the player repair a misunderstanding without losing the whole event?
- Observe target-language input/IME composition, slower typing, tab switching, interruption and reconnect. Record which clock was running and whether that consequence was understood.
- After losing or missing a visit, does the player know what remains available and want another attempt?
- Assess fun and perceived control separately from learning. Compare immediate recall with a delayed, different-context conversation using the same useful vocabulary; a successful transaction alone is not evidence of language acquisition.
